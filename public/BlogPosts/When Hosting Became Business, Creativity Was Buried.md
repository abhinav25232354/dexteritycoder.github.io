# I Thought My Website Would Pay for Itself. I Was Wrong.

I used to run a website called **dexteritycoder.com**, hosted on Hostinger's servers under their Premium Hosting Plan. I bought it with a simple belief: put in the work, build something real, and the internet would eventually reward that effort.

I paid around ₹2900 for a year of hosting. The math felt simple at the time: earn back more than I spent, then renew. But as anyone who has built something from scratch knows, the real world operates on a far more unforgiving set of rules than any algorithm you can write. I kept pushing content, articles, blog posts, projects with source code, portfolio pieces, and tutorials. I put genuine thought into every piece. What I did not account for was how long it would take for any of it to matter.

My site took nearly eight to nine months just to get indexed once. That single fact shattered everything I had assumed going in. And now, after all that waiting and building, my site has finally gone live on the internet only to find itself on the verge of dying because I do not have enough money to renew the hosting plan. I have created gigs on Fiverr to raise the funds. Nothing has worked. The feeling is not dramatic failure so much as a slow sinking, the kind where you keep paddling and the shore keeps moving. What makes it worse is knowing what a restart would cost, not money, but time. Another eight to nine months of invisibility. Another year of building into a void before anything surfaces.

## When Hosting Became a Business and Creativity Became a Cost

While sitting with all of this, I started thinking about how the web actually works now versus how it used to. Before hosting providers existed, people ran websites from their own machines. They pointed public IP addresses at home computers and learned enough about networking to keep things running. It was harder, but the barrier forced a certain kind of seriousness. People who published online did so because they had something worth publishing. The effort itself was a filter.

Then hosting became a product. Companies abstracted away the complexity and made it affordable. That part was genuinely good. But affordability came attached to a particular vision of what the web was for, and that vision was not creativity or knowledge sharing. It was traffic, search ranking, and SEO. Search Engine Optimization is not a bad idea in principle, but somewhere along the way it stopped being a tool and became the entire point. Writers now write for algorithms. Blogs are structured around keywords rather than ideas. Content that ranks is content that performs for a crawler, and the crawler does not care whether anyone actually learns something.

The result is visible every time you search for something specific and wade through page after page of optimized articles that restate the question without answering it. Google itself has been declining in usefulness for years, slowly, then all at once, and this is a significant reason why. A developer who has spent years building things and learning through direct experience will write differently than someone who learned a niche just well enough to stuff it with the right keywords. The first person writes with precision and specificity. The second writes for volume and coverage. Search engines, for a long time, could not tell the difference, and in many ways still cannot. So the person with real knowledge ends up buried under the person optimizing for rank.

## Where That Leaves Me

I built dexteritycoder.com because I wanted a place to put what I was actually learning and making. Not a portfolio dressed up as a blog, not keyword-optimized tutorials rewritten from documentation, but the real record of someone figuring things out in public. Eight to nine months of invisibility later, I am not sure the web has room for that anymore, at least not the web that runs on hosted servers and search engine traffic.

What I know is that starting over is not something I am ready to accept without a fight. The work is there. The content is indexed. The only thing standing between the site staying alive and the clock running out is a renewal fee I am still trying to find. If you have read this far, thank you. It means someone found this worth reading, and that is the only metric that ever actually mattered.
